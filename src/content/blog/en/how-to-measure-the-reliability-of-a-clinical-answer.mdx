The reliability of an AI-generated clinical answer cannot be judged by how it reads alone. An answer can sound correct and still omit a contraindication, mix levels of evidence or lose the operational context of a healthcare organisation.

The aim of an institutional platform is not to produce a brilliant answer in the abstract, but to deliver a conclusion that is **reviewable, traceable and useful** for teams working under clinical responsibility.

In a real care setting, a question rarely arrives on its own. It comes with context, time pressure, local documents, referral criteria, centre protocols and professional expectations. That is why evaluation has to look at both the final answer and the process that produces it: what information is retrieved, what is prioritised, what is left out and how uncertainty is expressed.

This perspective matters especially when the system is integrated into healthcare organisations with many teams. The same answer may be adequate for a general consultation but insufficient for a committee, an internal audit or a safety review. Measuring reliability means anticipating those uses and building operational evidence, not just a favourable impression.

## 1. Separate accuracy, traceability and usefulness

A mature evaluation distinguishes three dimensions that are often blurred together:

- **Clinical accuracy:** the answer is correct for the case in question.
- **Document traceability:** the system shows where each relevant statement comes from.
- **Operational usefulness:** the answer helps with the next decision without adding noise.

These three dimensions should be reviewed separately. An answer can be clinically reasonable but still insufficient if its source cannot be verified.

Clinical accuracy answers the question of whether the content is correct. Traceability answers whether it can be reviewed. Usefulness answers whether it helps move day-to-day practice forward. When one of these dimensions fails, trust weakens even if the others appear solid.

For example, an answer may correctly reflect a general recommendation but not state the document it comes from or the version used. In that case, the clinical team may sense that the answer is reasonable, but it does not have a sufficient basis to bring it into an institutional process. Conversely, an answer may cite its sources impeccably and still be of little use if it does not synthesise the next decision.

### Minimum evaluation criteria

Before accepting an answer as reliable, it is worth checking:

1. Whether it answers exactly the question asked.
2. Whether it states limits, uncertainties or conditions of application.
3. Whether it references verifiable content.
4. Whether it avoids extrapolating beyond the available corpus.
5. Whether its wording suits the care setting.

> Institutional trust is not built on a single answer, but on a system that can explain why it answers as it does and how it can be reviewed.

These criteria do not replace clinical judgement. They give it structure. They let different people review answers on a common basis and reduce reliance on subjective impressions such as ‘it sounds right’ or ‘it looks complete’. They also help to detect changes in behaviour when the document corpus is updated or internal priorities are changed.

## 2. Control questions to measure stability

Control questions make it possible to check that the system keeps its behaviour when documents, versions or clinical priorities change.

In practice, these questions act as a safety net. They do not try to cover every possible case, but to represent situations that are critical, frequent or especially sensitive. A good set of control questions includes simple questions, ambiguous questions and questions where the system has to recognise that it does not have enough information to answer safely.

The value of these tests shows over time. When a new protocol is added or a priority source is adjusted, control questions let you see whether the system improves, gets worse or changes unexpectedly. Without that measurement, every update to clinical knowledge becomes an act of trust that is hard to audit.

### Example of a basic matrix

- Frequent questions from everyday care.
- Safety-critical questions.
- Questions whose answer depends on age, comorbidity or context.
- Questions where the correct answer is to acknowledge that the information is insufficient.

#### Warning signs

A review should stop if patterns such as these appear:

- Answers more certain than the available evidence.
- Cited sources that do not support the conclusion.
- Omission of relevant warnings.
- Unjustified variability across equivalent questions.

These signs do not always point to a serious failure, but they do justify a review. Sometimes the problem lies in document retrieval, sometimes in the synthesis, and sometimes in the structure of the corpus itself. Evaluation has to make it possible to tell these causes apart, so as to avoid superficial fixes.

It is also important to keep a history of these findings. An evaluation record shows which problems have already been detected, what decisions were taken and which changes proved effective. In complex organisations, that record avoids repeating discussions and turns review into a cumulative process.

## 3. Expert review and the improvement cycle

Clinical review should not stop at saying whether an answer is right or wrong. It should leave a useful signal for improving the system:

1. Which part of the answer is correct.
2. Which part needs correcting.
3. Which source should be prioritised.
4. Which control question should be added to detect the problem in future.

The improvement cycle starts when expert review translates into concrete actions. If an answer omits a condition of application, it may be necessary to enrich the source document, adjust priorities or create a control question. If an answer cites an unrepresentative passage, the problem may not be the model but the way the corpus is segmented.

This approach also protects the clinical team from an excessive workload. The point is not to review everything continuously, but to create mechanisms that concentrate review where it adds most value: critical answers, document changes, frequent queries and situations with a higher risk of ambiguity.

### An evaluation that can be explained

Evaluation has to be communicable to different profiles: clinicians, systems managers, legal teams and healthcare management. Each profile needs a different level of detail, but all of them must be able to understand why an answer is considered acceptable or not.

That is why it is worth documenting the criteria in operational language. A platform may have sophisticated internal metrics, but if the result cannot be explained clearly, its institutional adoption will be more fragile.

---

## 4. Conclusion

Measuring reliability in medical AI requires combining clinical judgement, technical traceability and document governance. The result is not just a score: it is an institutional capacity to learn, correct and operate safely.

Reliability does not appear at the end of a project as a one-off validation. It is designed in from the start: in how content is structured, in how sources are retrieved, in the review criteria and in the discipline with which changes are recorded. When those pieces work together, AI stops being an isolated demonstration and becomes a governable tool within the health system.
